1 · The entropy bonus — what it actually does

1.1 Your intuition is right, but the mechanism is more specific

"Helps the model explore" is correct. The mechanism is: add η·H(π) to the actor's objective so that the loss has a term whose gradient pushes π toward higher entropy regardless of advantages.

ℒπ = −𝔼[ w · sg(A) · log π(a∣s) ]  −  η · H(π)
[ policy gradient ]   [ entropy bonus ]

Two distinct jobs:

TermWhat it doesWhat happens without it
−η·H(π)
(entropy bonus)		 Pushes π toward uniform. Always non-zero. Independent of returns. Acts as a floor on policy stochasticity. The policy collapses onto whichever action got even a slightly positive advantage early on. Once collapsed, π(a|s) ≈ 1 for one action, log π → 0, and the policy gradient term also vanishes. Dead policy. (This is exactly what happened in run 6nn4t1ky with η=3e-4.)
−w·sg(A)·log π
(policy gradient)		 Pushes probability mass toward actions with above-baseline returns and away from actions with below-baseline returns. Direction of "improvement." The policy stays uniform — exploration without learning. SR never rises above chance.

1.2 Why "exploration" is the right intuition but not the full story

In a max-entropy RL framing (Soft Actor-Critic style), the true objective is augmented:

J(π) = 𝔼[ Σt γt ( rt + α · H(π(·|st)) ) ]

i.e. the agent is rewarded for being uncertain. DreamerV3 uses a weaker form: η is small (3e-4 in the paper) so the entropy term is a regularizer rather than a true objective. It doesn't shape what the optimal policy looks like — it just prevents premature commitment.

Why a 4-action env needs more entropy than a 17-action env: the maximum entropy you can have is ln(num_actions): ln 4 ≈ 1.39 vs. ln 17 ≈ 2.83. With only 4 actions, even a small commitment to one of them collapses the entropy fast (going from 1.39 → 0.5 nats means one action at ~70%). Crafter has 17 actions; the same η contributes η·ln 17 instead of η·ln 4, so you can use a smaller η and still keep meaningful stochasticity. This is the structural reason the Habitat fork lifted η from 3e-4 → 3e-2.

2 · The policy gradient — purpose, and why "small" is correct

2.1 Purpose

The policy-gradient term is where the actual reinforcement learning happens. It's the answer to the question "given that I rolled out trajectory τ and observed advantages A, how should I change my policy?"

∇θ ℒPG = −𝔼[ A(s,a) · ∇θ log πθ(a∣s) ]

This is the standard REINFORCE / actor-critic gradient. Reading it directly:

  • A > 0 (action did better than expected) → push π(a∣s) up.
  • A < 0 (action did worse than expected) → push π(a∣s) down.
  • A = 0 (action met expectation) → no update.

2.2 Why the magnitude is small (and why that's fine)

R2-Dreamer normalises advantages by the percentile range of returns:

Â = sg(Rλ − V) / max(1, P95(R) − P5(R))

By construction:

  • The numerator is centred — average ≈ 0 across a batch (the value baseline soaks up the mean).
  • The denominator scales the spread to ~unit. |Â| is O(1), often O(0.1) when most actions are near-baseline.
  • log π(a∣s) ∈ [ln(unimix/4), 0]. With unimix_ratio=0.01, that's roughly [−4.6, 0], but for a near-uniform policy log π ≈ ln(1/4) = −1.39.
  • Product |Â · log π| ≈ 0.1 × 1.4 ≈ 0.14, but it averages to ~0 over a batch because 𝔼[Â] ≈ 0.

What we observe in a1aqhpnk: the PG contribution to loss/policy is ≈ ±0.005 (noise around zero), while the entropy bonus contributes a steady −0.040. So "small policy gradient" doesn't mean broken — it means the actor is near-equilibrium with the current critic baseline.

Schematic decomposition of loss/policy: the entropy bonus is a steady ground-state at ≈ −η·H, the PG term oscillates around zero, and their sum (the logged metric) sits just above the entropy line.

2.3 The asymmetry of jobs — a useful mental model

Policy gradientEntropy bonus
Magnitudenoisy, mean ≈ 0steady, large
Information content"which way to go""don't commit yet"
Cumulative effectdirectional drift over many batchesrestoring force back to uniform
If you remove itpolicy stays stochastic but learns nothing — chance-level performancepolicy collapses to argmax of early noise — locked-in bad policy

So the "small" PG isn't a bug — it's the integral of many small directional pushes. The entropy bonus is loud but informationless; the PG is quiet but informative.

3 · Three metrics for one number — what's actually different

Verified by subagent code audit (agent.py:680–702): the three metrics share a forward value but differ in two important ways.

MetricForward valueScale in total_lossGradient routeRole
loss/dyn KL[q‖p] 1.0 × loss posterior frozen; gradient → prior weights train the world model's prior to imagine the posterior
loss/rep KL[q‖p] 0.1 × loss prior frozen; gradient → encoder + posterior weights regularize the posterior toward predictable structure
latent/kl_divergence KL[q‖p] — (not in loss) no gradient — info metric only pure inspection

From config.py:64–65:

scale_dyn: float = 1.0   # full-strength
scale_rep: float = 0.1   # 10× weaker

And from agent.py:680–689:

total_loss = (
    cfg.scale_dyn * losses["dyn"]      # 1.0 × KL, gradient flows to PRIOR
  + cfg.scale_rep * losses["rep"]      # 0.1 × KL, gradient flows to POSTERIOR/ENCODER
  + ...
)

3.1 So why log all three?

  1. Gradient debugging. If loss/dyn is enormous but loss/rep is small, you'd realise the asymmetric gradient is producing different effective magnitudes — but they're the same scalar, so a single log would hide the issue. (In practice it never has — they're always identical because the forward KL is one tensor.)
  2. Architectural clarity. Logging both names documents that DreamerV3's KL has two roles. Removing either log would make the loss decomposition harder to read in W&B dashboards.
  3. Protection against bugs. If someone accidentally broke the stop-gradient symmetry (e.g., used a different KL direction in one of the two), the logged values would diverge and you'd see it immediately.
  4. latent/kl_divergence is purely cosmetic. It exists in the metrics namespace, not the loss namespace. It's a sanity probe. Could be removed without consequence.
Honest opinion: the loss/dyn + loss/rep double-log is justifiable for documentation; the latent/kl_divergence third log is redundant. If I were cleaning up the metric namespace, I'd drop latent/kl_divergence and keep the two loss-side logs.

3.2 Practical implication: loss/rep is the effective KL gradient on the encoder

Because scale_rep=0.1, only one-tenth of the KL gradient reaches the encoder/posterior. Doubling scale_rep would make the encoder more conservative (closer to prior); halving it would let the encoder become more reactive to observations. This is the canonical R2-Dreamer ratio (archiv/.../_base_.yaml:27–28 matches), so changing it is a research decision, not a tuning knob.

5 · Crafter A/B — what to compare and why

Goal: prove that the JAX fork reproduces the upstream PyTorch on a standardised benchmark (Crafter). This is the single most useful sanity check before running any 3D-feature experiments. If they don't match on Crafter with matched configs, every Habitat result is suspect.

Three seeds per cell = 9 runs total. With Crafter's ~1M-step length, each run is ~6–8 hours on an H100. Fits in one weekend on bwUniCluster.

5.2 Success criteria

  • upstream-pt achieves Crafter score ≥ 13 (paper reports 14.5 ± 1.2 for R2-Dreamer 12M).
  • fork-jax-paperent within 1 std of upstream-pt on (a) Crafter score, (b) episode reward, (c) entropy trajectory. This is the port-equivalence pass criterion.
  • fork-jax-habitatent Crafter score is lower than fork-jax-paperent — confirms 3e-2 is too aggressive on a 17-action env (the symmetric outcome to v5's 3e-4 collapse on 4-action Habitat).
