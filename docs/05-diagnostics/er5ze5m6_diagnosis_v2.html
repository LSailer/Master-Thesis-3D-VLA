1 — What does actor/entropy mean and what does it tell you?

The actor entropy is the Shannon entropy of the categorical policy at a state, averaged over the training batch:

H[π(·|s)]  =  − Σa π(a|s) · log π(a|s)  ∈  [ 0,  log N ]

For 4 ObjectNav actions: H = log 4 ≈ 1.386 when uniform, 0 when deterministic. That single scalar tells you four different things at four different points in training:

What you see in actor/entropyWhat it means
Decreases smoothly from ≈ log N toward a small positive value (e.g. 0.5–0.8) Healthy training. Policy is sharpening on the actions that matter, but still exploring.
Stuck at log N for many gradient steps Either (a) η is too large and the entropy term dominates, or (b) the advantage signal is zero (no λ-return variance, or Anorm ≈ 0). Need to look at actor/advantage_std to disambiguate.
Collapses to ≈ 0 quickly Mode-collapse. Actor commits to one action — usually because η is too small or advantage normalization doesn't fire and a noisy return spike pulls all logit mass to one action. This is what llspyx9j did at the action level.
Oscillates / climbs back up Policy is unstable; either critic is lagging behind (try slow-target update) or returns are exploding (check ret_scale).

What you can infer if entropy is unavailable (this project's situation): the per-episode action shares are a noisy proxy. er5ze5m6's 0.21/0.25/0.29/0.25 has Shannon entropy ≈ 1.379 (i.e. ~99.5% of log 4) — close to maximally entropic. lhgoxh0y's 0.25/0.27/0.25/0.24 is essentially the same. But llspyx9j's 0.98/0.00/0.01/0.00 has entropy ≈ 0.10 — almost deterministic. Per-episode action shares can diagnose collapse but cannot diagnose "stuck at log N". That is exactly why actor/entropy at the gradient-step level is the key missing metric.

3 — What is A_norm? How does the code match Eq. 6?

The actor objective in DreamerV3 (Hafner et al. 2023, p. 6, Eq. 6, verbatim from the arXiv PDF):

L(θ) = − Σt=1T  sg( (Rtλ − vψ(st)) / max(1, S) )  log πθ(at | st)  +  η · H[πθ(at | st)] — Hafner et al. 2023, "Actor learning", §3, Eq. 6 (arXiv:2301.04104, p. 6)

The bracketed term being multiplied by log π is the normalized advantage:

Anorm(st)  =  sg( (Rtλ − vψ(st)) / max(1, S) )
where Rtλ is the λ-return, vψ the critic, and S = EMA0.99( Per(Rλ, 95) − Per(Rλ, 5) )

The sg(·) is stop-gradient — the actor sees the advantage as a constant weight on its log-prob, gradients only flow through log π. The denominator max(1, S) is the key DreamerV3 innovation: it scales down large returns but leaves small returns alone, so a single fixed η works across domains.

3.1 — Side-by-side: paper vs this codebase

Paper (Eq. 6)

L = − Σt sg( (Rλ−v) / max(1,S) ) · log π  +  η · H[π]

Sign convention: loss is minimised → +η·H means entropy is rewarded.

Code (agent.py:532–535)

losses["policy"] = jnp.mean(
  sg(weight[:, :-1])
  * -(logpi * sg(adv) + cfg.act_entropy * entropy)
)

Distributing the outer minus: L = mean(weight · (−adv·logπ − η·H)) — sign-equivalent to the paper.

3.2 — Theoretical range of Anorm in this project

Reward shaping: step_penalty = −0.01, success_bonus = +10, max_episode_steps = 500. Discount γ = 1 − 1/333 ≈ 0.997. So:

  • Best-case episode return (success in 1 step): Rλ ≈ 9.99.
  • Worst-case (timeout): −0.01 × 500 ≈ −5.0.
  • Per-step return after λ-bootstrap: typically in [−2, +5] except on success steps.

The percentile width S = Per(R, 95) − Per(R, 5) on this distribution settles to ≈ 3–7 once the agent is reaching some chairs. Then A_norm = (R − v) / S typically lives in [−0.5, +0.5] with mode near 0 (because the critic predicts most steps' return well).

4 — Why is the policy-gradient term so small? (Prognosis 2 in 5 figures)

Prognosis 2 from v1: tiny advantage signal vs. dominant entropy term. The chain that makes the policy-gradient (PG) term small:

1) reward is mostly −0.01 with rare +10 spikes  →
2) λ-return distribution is narrow with a tail  →
3) percentile S ≥ 1 normalizes returns to roughly [−1, +1]  →
4) advantage A_norm = (R − v)/max(1,S) is mode-zero, std small  →
5) PG gradient ∝ A_norm · ∇log π; entropy gradient ∝ η · ∇H.
At η = 3·10⁻² and σ(A) ≈ 0.05, the entropy gradient is ~10× larger than the PG gradient.

Q-answer: yes, the negative-reward dominance is the reason

You asked: "is this because most reward steps are negative?" — yes, that's the proximate cause. With −0.01 on 99% of steps and +10 on 0.1%, the critic v(s) learns to predict ≈ −3 nearly everywhere; R − v is then mostly noise in [−0.5, +0.5] after percentile normalisation. The Hafner paper anticipates exactly this:

"The actor learns to choose actions that maximize return while exploring through an entropy regularizer. However, the correct scale for this regularizer depends both on the scale and frequency of rewards in the environment. Ideally, we would like the agent to explore more if rewards are sparse and exploit more if rewards are dense or nearby. At the same time, the exploration amount should not be influenced by arbitrary scaling of rewards in the environment. This requires normalizing the return scale while preserving information about reward frequency." — Hafner et al. 2023, p. 6, "Actor learning"

5 — actent = 3·10⁻⁴ in the paper vs. this project

The paper recommends η = 3·10⁻⁴ fixed across all domains (Tab. 4, p. 21):

"To use a fixed entropy scale of η = 3 × 10⁻⁴ across domains…"
"The same values are used across all benchmarks, including proprioceptive and visual inputs, continuous and discrete actions, and 2D and 3D domains. We do not use any hyperparameter annealing, prioritized replay, weight decay, or dropout." — Hafner et al. 2023, p. 6 + Table 4 caption, p. 21

The paper's prescription for sparse-reward tasks is not to raise η. It is to fix η and rely on the percentile normalizer with the L = 1 floor:

"To avoid amplifying noise from function approximation under sparse rewards, we only scale down large return magnitudes but leave small returns below the threshold of L = 1 untouched… exploring rapidly under sparse rewards and converging to high performance across diverse domains." — Hafner et al. 2023, p. 6, "Actor learning"

The team raised η by 100× because 3·10⁻⁴ caused collapse on 4-action HM3D — which llspyx9j just reproduced. So the empirical finding is real and reproducible. But it is also evidence that something else in the pipeline is not behaving paper-faithfully, because the paper's claim is that fixed η works on all domains given the percentile normalizer.

Hypothesis worth checking: the percentile normalizer's L = 1 floor in this code applies to the *width*, not to the absolute scale. With a 4-action policy and 99% −0.01 rewards, returns may be small enough in absolute value that the normalizer never gets a chance to fire (S stays near 1), but the critic still produces v ≈ −3, so R − v has a huge negative bias. Logging actor/return_mean, actor/value_mean, and actor/ret_scale would settle this.

5.2 — Reading η as a thermostat, not a tuning knob

  • η = 3·10⁻⁴ on a well-normalized return: PG term dominates, actor learns sharply.
  • η = 3·10⁻⁴ on a poorly normalized return (this project): both PG and entropy gradients are tiny; whichever has more variance wins, and at low η it's the noisy PG → collapse.
  • η = 3·10⁻² on the same return: entropy term floors exploration, the actor stays "warm" long enough for the WM to see varied data → it learns slower but does learn.

6 — Five ways to extend your "Crafter vs. ours" notebook

You already diff this fork against the Crafter-original DreamerV3. Here are five concrete next-step analyses, ordered by expected payoff.

  1. Compare the actor-loss block line-for-line. Open danijar/dreamerv3/dreamerv3/agent.py and modules/r2dreamer/agent.py:512–535. Specifically check: (a) is the offset Per5 subtracted before division? (b) is weight a discount cumulative or a binary mask in Crafter? (c) is the entropy weighted by weight or by a separate term? Any deviation goes into the notebook as a row of "differences that could change the gradient".
  2. Replay buffer & is_first audit. The replay sampler decides whether the WM ever sees temporally-coherent windows. Compare modules/shared/replay_buffer.py:85–87 against the Crafter reference. Two things to verify: (i) the is_first flag is set on the post-reset step, not the pre-reset step (one-off bug); (ii) sequences that span two episodes are correctly masked so the RSSM resets mid-window.
  3. Critic / value-loss formulation. DreamerV3 uses a two-hot symlog value head with slow target. Diff scale_value, scale_repval, twohot_bins, slow_target_fraction against the Crafter defaults. The fact that loss/value in llspyx9j is 1.10 (vs 1.52 in lhgoxh0y) despite the WM being much worse is suspicious — it suggests the critic is fitting a degenerate target.
  4. Reward-prediction sanity check. In a notebook cell: load a checkpoint, sample 10 000 replay states, run the reward head, plot r̂ vs r. If r̂ is essentially flat at −0.01 the reward head has memorized the marginal — which means the WM cannot guide the actor toward the +10 spikes. This is a more useful test than loss/rew (which can look "fine" while predicting only the mean).
  5. Crafter-shaped reward sanity run. Crafter's rewards are dense and small (≤ 1 per achievement). The single most informative ablation is to run this codebase on Crafter without changing any hyperparameter and see whether SR matches the paper. If yes, the implementation is paper-faithful and the gap is reward-shape; if no, you've localized a real bug.
